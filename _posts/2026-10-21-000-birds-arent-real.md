In the early second century, the Roman poet Juvenal wrote a series of satires about the city around him. They are furious, funny, and frequently unfair. He went after corrupt patrons, social climbers, hypocritical moralists, the noise and danger of Roman streets, and the poverty of anyone who tried to make a living with their mind. Very little is known about the man himself, but his poems survived because readers kept copying them -- through late antiquity, into the monastic libraries of medieval Europe, and on into the schoolrooms of the Renaissance. Several of his phrases are still in common use. "Bread and games" is his. So is "Who will watch the watchmen?"

Satire VII is about intellectuals: poets who depend on patrons, historians nobody pays, teachers whose fees get haggled down by parents. A few manage to prosper, Juvenal concedes, but they owe it to luck, and a man that lucky is "rarer than a white raven." He used a similar bird elsewhere. In Satire VI, an uncharitable portrait of Roman wives, he called a truly virtuous woman _rara avis in terris nigroque simillima cycno_: a rare bird on earth, very like a black swan. In both poems the bird means the same thing: something so unlikely that, for practical purposes, it doesn't exist.

Neither bird was Juvenal's invention. Aristotle, ostensibly trying to account for sightings of a white raven, suggested a hard winter could make them so. Lucretius, arguing that atoms have no colour, offered white ravens and black swans as two things nature plainly never makes -- Juvenal's exact pair, a century and a half earlier. Cicero, writing under Caesar's dictatorship, complained that a loyal citizen had become as rare as a white bird. Ovid told how the raven had been white until Apollo blackened it as punishment for gossip. By Juvenal's day, the birds were clichés. What set Juvenal apart was what happened to his poems afterwards. Lucretius spent the Middle Ages in a handful of manuscripts and was barely read until a humanist found a copy in 1417. Cicero's letters fared little better. Juvenal was copied again and again -- more than five hundred medieval manuscripts survive -- and taught in the schools where the few who could read learned their Latin. The clichés weren't his, but for centuries his were the lines people quoted.

Fifteen centuries later, one of those birds turned up.

## The Swan River

In the late 1600s, the Dutch East India Company, known as the VOC, was the most powerful trading company in the world. Its Asian headquarters was Batavia, on the island of Java, the city now called Jakarta. Every year, Company ships left the Netherlands loaded with soldiers, sailors, officials, settlers, and silver, and made the long voyage to Batavia by way of the Cape of Good Hope.

From the Cape, Company rules required captains to take a particular route. Rather than creeping up the coast of Africa, they dropped south into the strong westerly winds below the Cape, ran east across the Indian Ocean, and turned north toward Java. The route cut months off the voyage. It also had a flaw. Sailors of the time had no reliable way to measure longitude, so a captain could only estimate how far east he'd come. Turn north too late, and the next thing ahead was the western coast of an unmapped continent the Dutch called the South Land -- what is now Australia. Several VOC ships had already been wrecked there.

In July 1693, the _Ridderschap van Holland_ left the Netherlands on what should have been a routine voyage. She was a large ship, built in Amsterdam, and she had made the trip four times before. She was armed with 36 guns to defend herself from rampant piracy. Carrying around 300 people, she reached the Cape in January 1694, took on supplies, and sailed again on 5 February. She was never seen again.

There were two theories on her disappearance. One held that she'd been taken by pirates near Madagascar. The other, favored by Company officials, was that she had followed the route east, turned north too late, and run aground on the South Land, where survivors might still be waiting. Nicolaes Witsen, burgomaster of Amsterdam and one of the Company's directors, pressed for a rescue mission. The man chosen to lead it was Willem de Vlamingh, a former whaling captain from the island of Vlieland.

Vlamingh left the Netherlands in May 1696 with three ships: his own frigate, the _Geelvinck_, and two smaller vessels, the _Nijptangh_ and the _Weseltje_. The Netherlands was at war with France, so the little fleet sailed around the north of Scotland to avoid French privateers. Scurvy broke out on the way, and they spent seven weeks recovering at the Cape. Then they followed the route the _Ridderschap_ had taken, stopping to search two remote islands in the southern Indian Ocean. They found nothing.

They reached the South Land at the end of December 1696, nearly eight months out of the Netherlands, and found a low, dry coast with no sign of a wreck. On 10 January 1697, Vlamingh sent three boats up a broad river near what is now Perth, to search, with the hope of finding the peoples whose fires they had seen burning further inland.

A journal kept aboard the _Nijptangh_, printed in Amsterdam a few years later, records this journey. At daybreak on the 11th the boats pushed upriver and, the writer noted, "saw many swans (our boat knocked over nine or ten)." Those swans were black. The birds Juvenal and the rest of antiquity had used to describe something that could not exist were so thick on the water that the boats couldn't avoid running into them. The crews rowed on until the river grew too shallow to follow, finding huts and the remains of fires but never the people who had made them. "The men, the birds, the swans, the rotganzen, koopganzen, the geese, the cockatoos, the parroquets, etc.," the journal says, "all fled at the sight of us."

<aside class="note pull" aria-hidden="true">Nobody aboard wrote that a fifteen-hundred-year-old proverb had just died.</aside>

What the journal doesn't record is any sense of occasion. Nobody aboard wrote that a fifteen-hundred-year-old proverb had just died. They were eight months from home on a rescue mission that was already failing. Somewhere on this coast, if the Company was right, were three hundred of their countrymen. Somebody inland had lit those fires. For them, after a harrowing, months-long journey around a war, through a scurvy epidemic, and into pirate-infested waters, the swans were just birds: some to eat, perhaps a few to take back alive. Vlamingh, recognizing their prevalence if not their significance, named the river after them, the _Zwaanenrivier_ (Swan River), and the expedition moved on up the coast, still looking for survivors.

It wasn't the first time a European had seen a black swan. Sixty years earlier, in 1636, a Company sailor named Antonie Caen had recorded black birds "as large as swans" farther up the same coast. His sighting went where a trading company's observations usually did: into a log, and from there into the archives. An English ambassador of the period once claimed that the VOC suppressed news of the South Land to keep rivals away. A nineteenth-century historian of the voyages offered a duller explanation: Dutch travel accounts were published for a popular market, and a voyage without strange adventures had little hope of finding readers. Either way, the result was the same. Charts of a dangerous coast, and tales of derring-do had value. Strange birds did not.

Vlamingh's voyage left a better record than Caen's. It had an influential sponsor who wanted a full report. It carried Victor Victorszoon, who tended the sick aboard the _Geelvinck_ and painted the coastlines and anchorages in watercolour as they went. And its results were bound for the learned societies of Europe as well as the Company's offices. The swans were the same birds Caen had seen. The difference between the two sightings was the context: what questions were being asked, by whom, why, and at what cost. Details like these often decide what counts as worthwhile or notable, and sometimes what gets accepted as true.

Vlamingh also brought back what a careful observer should: three black swans, alive. The Company's officials in Batavia reported that they "should have been sent to Your Nobilities had they not died one by one shortly after their arrival here." The same report summed up the voyage: a barren coast, no trace of the missing ship, and "nothing of any importance" discovered. Witsen passed the results on to the Royal Society in London. The swans got a single line, in a list with parrots and sea cows, and Witsen concluded that nothing had been found "which can be any way serviceable to the Company."

Most of Victorszoon's work to illustrate the voyage was lost to time. An engraving based on one of his drawings, not published until the 1720s, shows the expedition's ships at anchor with black swans drifting in the foreground. A set of his watercolours didn't turn up until 1970 when they were discovered in a Rotterdam museum. 

## Who could have seen this coming?

Philosophers eventually got the most use out of the black swan. In formal logic, it became the standard example of a hard limit on knowledge: no number of white swans, as Karl Popper later put it, can prove that all swans are white, and one black swan proves they aren't. But outside of lecture halls, the new bird made almost no difference at all.

More than a century after Vlamingh sailed a fleet there, a natural history compiled in New South Wales in 1813 described the black swan as a bird whose existence "by thousands is still doubted." In 1835 the French Academy's dictionary still defined the swan as a large water bird with white plumage, adding as an afterthought that "in New Holland there are black swans." Live ones had reached England around 1791. By 1851 a pair could be bought at Leadenhall Market in London, and they were soon breeding on the River Wandle in Surrey. Yet people went on saying "black swan" to mean something that couldn't exist.

When the phrase finally caught up, it changed jobs rather than retiring. "Black swan" stopped meaning something that cannot happen and came to mean something nobody saw coming, the sense Nassim Taleb made famous in 2007 and the one risk managers use today. The discovery of the bird didn't kill the idiom. It became the idiom.

The white raven had an easier time, because it was never quite wrong. White ravens are, in fact, real, but exceedingly rare. A raven can be white only when it inherits the same uncommon recessive gene from both of its black parents. Yet their rarity goes beyond the odds of a genetic lottery. An albino raven lacks pigment entirely and has pink eyes and poor eyesight. A leucistic bird suffers from lack of pigmentation, losing most of its colour but retaining dark or blue eyes. Both types of birds stands out to anything that may hunt them, and their feathers are weaker; the pigment that makes a raven black also makes its feathers tough. So even those ravens that hatch with genetic abnormalities have significant challenges to overcome in order to reach adulthood. When two pairs of ordinary black ravens on Vancouver Island were spotted raising white chicks a few years ago, it made the news. The "white raven" still means what Juvenal meant: something too rare to count on. The phrase survives because it never claimed anything anyone could disprove.

## Which bird?

The image of a white bird as the definition of rarity spread across Europe. The bird didn't travel as well.

German says _ein weißer Rabe_, a white raven. Polish says _biały kruk_, which today usually means a rare book; the great Polish dictionary of the early 1800s listed "black swan, white raven" together as examples of the impossible. Russian chose the crow, _белая ворона_, and over time the phrase drifted from meaning a rarity to meaning a misfit. As early as 1694, the French Academy's dictionary recorded a retort for an impossible boast -- if you can do that, I'll give you a white blackbird. Italian gave up on birds and settled for _una mosca bianca_, a white fly.

The idea survived everywhere. The bird was always negotiable.

English couldn't decide either. In English, "crow" has long served as the general name for the whole family of large black birds, with "raven" reserved for the biggest of them. So most English translations of Juvenal turned his white raven into a white crow, even though the Latin word, _corvus_, is the raven. The translators weren't exactly wrong. In English, a raven is a kind of crow. When English-speaking logicians needed a universal statement nobody would dispute, they reached for the same bird, and "all crows are black" became a stock example in textbooks on reasoning.

In the 1940s, the bird changed again, and this time the change mattered.

## The Raven Paradox

Carl Gustav Hempel was born near Berlin in 1905. He finished his doctorate in 1934, a year into Nazi rule, and left soon after; his wife was of Jewish descent. By 1937 he was in the United States. He went on to teach at Yale and Princeton, and his account of what it means for science to explain something became the standard one for a generation.

The question that made him famous sounds almost childish: what makes something count as evidence for a hypothesis? Take the hypothesis that all ravens are black. Nobody can check it directly. There are too many ravens, spread too widely, and more hatch every spring. What anyone can do is look at ravens one at a time. Each black one seems to make the hypothesis a little more believable, though no number of them can prove it. Hempel set out to define, with the precision of logic, exactly when an observation supports a hypothesis and when it doesn't. It seemed like it should be easy. It wasn't.

His first paper on the problem, in 1943, used the old standby: all swans are white. Two years later, in the paper that made the problem famous, the swans were gone, replaced by "all ravens are black."

<aside class="note pull" aria-hidden="true">Maybe he simply noticed that swans make an awkward example for a theory of how evidence supports a belief, when the belief in question had been famously false since 1697.</aside>

He never explained the change. Hempel grew up speaking German, where the bird of idiom is the raven, and he had studied Juvenal's Latin at university in Berlin. Maybe he simply noticed that swans make an awkward example for a theory of how evidence supports a belief, when the belief in question had been famously false since 1697. Whatever the reason, the puzzle he published in 1945 became known as Hempel's paradox, or more commonly "The Raven Paradox." It remains one of the most famous problems in the philosophy of science, taught to generations of students and still argued over, because it goes straight to what it means for anything to count as proof. Juvenal would have recognized the bird.

## Indoor ornithology

The paradox starts from something that seems obvious: seeing a black raven supports the claim that all ravens are black. But "all ravens are black" says exactly the same thing as "anything that isn't black isn't a raven." The two sentences are one claim worded two ways; they're true or false together. And the second wording is supported by anything that is neither black nor a raven -- a green leaf, to use one of Hempel's examples. If evidence for one wording is evidence for the other, then the leaf supports the claim about ravens, and so does nearly everything else in the world that isn't black. A person could, it seemed, study birds without leaving the house. The philosopher Nelson Goodman called it "indoor ornithology."

Later retellings replaced Hempel's examples with a white shoe, and that's the version most people know. The birds weren't the only thing that changed in transmission.

That's the paradox. Two rules that each seem plainly right -- an observation that fits a hypothesis supports it, and whatever supports a hypothesis also supports any other way of stating the same hypothesis -- combine to produce a conclusion that seems plainly absurd. Something has to give. Either one of the rules is wrong, or the absurd conclusion is actually true. Philosophers have spent the better part of a century arguing about which.

Hempel's own answer was that the conclusion is true. The leaf only seems irrelevant, he argued, because we already know it isn't a raven. Someone judging the leaf on its own, knowing nothing else, would have to count it as a sliver of evidence. As pure logic, that may be right. That isn't how human cognition works. Nobody judges anything with a blank mind. People weigh what they see against everything they already believe, and what they already believe decides where they bother to look.

For fifteen centuries, Europe's knowledge of swans was indoor ornithology. Every swan anyone in Europe had seen was white, and each one seemed to confirm the proverb. But no amount of looking at European swans could have turned up a black one: there weren't any to find. The birds that could have changed anyone's mind were on the far side of the world, and the men who finally rowed among them had other things on their minds. They were searching for three hundred missing people. A black swan had nothing to say about where those people were, so it was a bird, and the boats rowed on.

## One white raven

There's no straight line from Juvenal's raven to Hempel's. There are several overlapping histories, and at nearly every turn, something changed. A raven became a crow in English. Swans became ravens between one of Hempel's papers and the next. A leaf became a shoe. And the bird that should have retired the oldest proverb about impossibility was noted, eaten, given a river, and filed.

So why did the proverb survive? And why do we still say it?

Part of the answer is that most of what anyone knows came from someone else, and it rarely feels that way. Psychologists call this the illusion of explanatory depth. Ask people how well they understand a zipper, a flush toilet, or a door lock, and most will say pretty well, until they're asked to explain how one actually works. People routinely mistake knowing that someone understands a thing for understanding it themselves.

That's how Europe knew swans were white. No one had surveyed the world's swans. Juvenal said so, the proverb said so, and every swan on every European pond agreed. When word came that swans could be black, the people who accepted it accepted it the same way: someone credible said so. Either way, the knowledge belonged to somebody else, and it felt like everyone's own.

Repetition made it sturdier. People judge a familiar statement more likely to be true simply because they've heard it before, and experiments have found that this holds even when they know better. A phrase that's easy to say is easy to believe. "Black swan" had been the easy way to say "impossible" for seventeen centuries. A report from the far side of the world was never going to compete.

A belief that has been put to work is also very hard to dislodge. When researchers tell people that the evidence behind a belief was invented, the belief usually survives, and it survives best in people who have used it to explain something. Europe had been using the white swan to explain things since Lucretius built an argument about atoms on it. Nobody pulls out a load-bearing wall on the strength of a report from the other side of the world.

So the belief got the smallest repair possible. The dictionary kept the swan white and added a note about New Holland. The idiom kept its words and swapped its meaning. The white raven held onto its rarity and quietly let go of the bird. Nobody had to admit being wrong, and nothing nearby had to move.

The rest of the answer is one the sailors would have understood. Whether a fact matters has little to do with whether it's accurate. It depends on who finds it, what they were looking for, and what it would cost them to ignore it. The Company needed charts, so the coast got charted. The men on the river were looking for their countrymen, so the swans were dinner. And nobody in Europe lost anything by calling a black swan impossible, so nobody had a reason to stop.

All of this is ordinary. Nobody can check everything, and trusting people who know more, repeating what everyone says, and leaving alone what still seems to work are how anyone gets through a day. Together, though, those habits let a belief be borrowed, felt as one's own, disproven, and kept, without a seam showing. Where nothing much rides on the belief, that's harmless. Where a great deal does, it's foolish, however common it is.

In 1789, nearly a century after Vlamingh, the published account of the first governor of New South Wales described the black swan as a bird that, "though proverbially rare in other parts of the world, is here by no means uncommon." On the other side of the world it was still a figure of speech. Both were describing what they could see, and both were right.

That's where everyone stands on nearly everything: beside the few rivers they know, and trusting of those further afield on the rest. The beliefs that deserve the most attention are seldom the ones people are unsure of. They're the ones nobody thinks to ask about, borrowed so long ago they feel original, repeated until they're effortless, and patched so quietly that no one remembers the repair. Some of them are true. From where most of us stand, the true and the patched look exactly the same. 

Those who bother to make their way to the waterfront know better.
