## The origins of technological insecurity

Breaches are reported as technical events. The conditions that made them possible often developed long before the technical failure. It might be a decision made on a metric no one questioned. It might be a control that existed on paper but not in practice. It might be a team that learned to satisfy the audit instead of the risk the audit was meant to address.

Most assessments start at the technical layer and treat people, culture, and incentives as background. We reverse that order. Our primary subjects are:

- how people think and decide
- institutional culture
- incentives and authority
- the way an organization produces knowledge about itself

The technical work then tests what those conditions have produced.

## The limits of institutional knowledge

No organization has a complete picture of how its systems operate, how its people behave, or how its decisions will play out. Instead, it relies on representations of those things: risk registers, maturity scores, control attestations, threat models, and the judgments of people entrusted with interpreting them.

These representations are indispensable. They are also incomplete. The difficulty begins when the distinction between a representation and the reality it describes becomes hard to see, or inconvenient to acknowledge.

Research into auditing has shown how organizations change their behavior to satisfy the demands of verification, sometimes at the expense of the work being verified. Investigations into the Challenger disaster revealed how engineering anomalies came to be accepted as normal through organizational pressures and a succession of decisions that appeared reasonable at the time.

People also adapt to the systems meant to govern them. Teams learn what a metric rewards and deliver exactly that. Workarounds become standard practice. Eventually the policy describes an organization that no longer exists.

Complexity makes all of this worse. Once systems outgrow anyone's ability to inspect them directly, decision-makers rely on intermediaries: dashboards, vendor claims, and automated interpretations. Those intermediaries can acquire an authority their underlying evidence doesn't justify. The drift that follows is familiar:

- A metric becomes a target, and the target becomes the purpose.
- A framework adopted to satisfy a regulator becomes the definition of the program.
- Whatever can't be measured falls out of the budget, and then out of the conversation.

The result can be an organization that describes its security posture fluently and is still wrong about it.

## Automating judgment under uncertainty

Artificial intelligence and increasingly autonomous systems introduce a new dimension to an old problem: organizations can now delegate interpretation, decisions, and actions to systems operating on assumptions they may not fully understand.

These systems operate within organizations whose taxonomies, permissions, policies, and incentives already reflect years of accumulated decisions and assumptions. Some of those assumptions are encoded directly into the technology; others influence how it is deployed, supervised, and evaluated.

Automation can give these assumptions consequences at a different scale. A classification that once influenced a quarterly report might now determine thousands of automated actions. Those actions may themselves generate reports, assessments, and recommendations consumed by other systems, creating additional distance between a decision and the evidence on which it rests.

Before delegating judgment or action to AI, it's worth knowing which beliefs you're encoding into it.
{: .turn}

## Where we come in

Our work is most useful when an organization faces a consequential decision and needs more than another assessment of its existing controls.

That might mean challenging the assumptions behind a major technology investment, examining why an incident occurred despite apparently adequate safeguards, or investigating the gap between what leadership has been told and what people operating the systems actually experience.

It can also mean examining an organization before anything has gone wrong. A security program can be well managed, thoroughly documented, and entirely sincere in its conclusions while still overlooking something important.

## What we investigate

A conventional red team tests whether controls hold against an adversary. We treat the organization as a sociotechnical system and test whether its understanding of itself holds against evidence. That means asking:

- What does leadership believe about its exposure, its dependencies, and its ability to respond, and where did each belief come from?
- Which failures do the organization's incentives make difficult to see or impossible to report?
- Where has complexity replaced independent judgment with trust in a tool, a vendor, or a score?
- Which metrics and abstractions are driving decisions, and do they measure what people assume they measure?
- How has actual behavior adapted around policy and controls, and what openings does that create for an attacker?

The work combines technical testing, review of documents and data, and interviews across levels of the organization. It draws on security engineering and hacker culture alongside sociology, psychology, economics, organizational research, philosophy, and the history of technology.

Throughout, we hold to a few distinctions: evidence versus measurement, knowledge versus representation, and security versus the institutional appearance of security.

## Independence

We don't sell or resell software, and we take no referral fees. No platform is waiting at the end of an engagement.

We also don't assume that more controls, metrics, technology, or organizational complexity count as improvement. Sometimes the most useful finding is something to remove.

Sometimes the evidence supports what you're already doing, and we'll say so.

## On the name

For centuries, ravens have been associated with the Tower of London. According to legend, six ravens must remain there; should they ever leave, the Tower will fall, and the kingdom with it.

The origins of the legend are less certain than its telling. The ravens remain.

We know better than to mistake a good story for evidence. We also know better than to underestimate the power of one.
