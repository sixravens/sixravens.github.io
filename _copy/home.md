## How we think

Technology doesn't operate independently of the people and institutions that build and use it. A missed alert may have less to do with the detection than with an analyst who saw it forty times last week. A misconfiguration may have been a reasonable decision by someone working with incomplete information. A control may have passed every assessment without anyone questioning what it was accomplishing.

> Fix the alert, correct the configuration, replace the control. Sometimes that's enough. Often the conditions that produced the failure remain.

We follow those conditions into the decisions, incentives, assumptions, and habits that shape how an organization operates. The work draws on security engineering, but also on disciplines that have spent much longer studying how people think and act.

### AI is not a separate problem

Artificial intelligence gives these questions new consequences. Organizations are granting autonomous systems access and authority that once belonged to people, often without the same ability to understand, challenge, or interrupt their decisions. The technology is new. The underlying problems of trust, judgment, and accountability are not.

## Selected reading

From [*one white raven*](owr), a publication of Six Ravens.

*One White Raven* investigates how people create, interact with, and adapt to complex systems—and how those interactions shape what we believe, how we behave, and what ultimately happens. We draw on research across disciplines to examine these relationships, particularly where technology and artificial intelligence are changing their nature.

## When we're useful

Most engagements begin when an organization is building out a security capability, doubts an assurance that looks better on paper than in practice, or has contained an incident it doesn’t yet understand. AI is increasingly part of each: in the tools doing the detecting, the systems being given authority, and the adversaries on the other side.

Whether building or scaling detection and response, running an exercise, or investigating an incident, the work starts with the business: the systems that matter, the people who keep them secure, and what they can observe, investigate, and decide when something goes wrong. No framework or taxonomy knows any of that about your organization. Every detection should land with someone who knows what it means and can act on it. Every response should be steady enough that people can do their jobs instead of reacting to every alarm. We build toward those moments, rehearse them, and reconstruct them when they fail.

A critical vulnerability is disclosed. A vendor issues an advisory. An executive forwards an article. In a lot of organizations, each one turns into a two-week scramble that pulls people off the work that would actually make the organization safer. That reflex has causes: incentives, accountability, competition for attention, and the fear of being the person who didn’t act. We spend a lot of time studying them. We help organizations build the judgment to decide what deserves a response, and the standing to say so. Programs improve in the quiet stretches between emergencies. We help create more of them.

### Intentionally biased

We’re small by design. We only accept focused engagements, don’t operate a SOC or provide emergency incident response, and we don’t sell security products or collect referral fees.
